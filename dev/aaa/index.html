The AAA fit

AAA() finds zeros of $f$ by sampling $f$ on a grid $z_1,\dots,z_m$ and fitting a rational function to $y_i = 1/f(z_i)$. It reads the zeros of $f$ off as the poles of the fit; each candidate is then confirmed by the winding on a small circle around, and polished. This page describes the fit and what it costs.

The AAA iteration

AAA [1] writes the fit in barycentric form over $n$ support points $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n$ chosen among the samples,

\[r(z) = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n} \dfrac{w_k\, y_{\sigma_k}}{z - z_{\sigma_k}}} {\sum_{k=1}^{n} \dfrac{w_k}{z - z_{\sigma_k}}} ,\]

a rational function of degree $n-1$ that interpolates $y$ at the support points for any nonzero weights $w$. The weights are free to make $r$ match the other samples. Multiplying $y_i - r(z_i)$ by the denominator makes the mismatch linear in $w$: it is $(Lw)_i$ with the Loewner matrix

\[L_{ik} = \frac{y_i - y_{\sigma_k}}{z_i - z_{\sigma_k}}, \qquad i \notin \sigma ,\]

so $w$ is taken as the unit vector minimizing $\lVert Lw\rVert$, the right singular vector of the smallest singular value. The iteration is greedy: the sample where $|y_i - r(z_i)|$ is largest becomes the next support point, until the mismatch falls below tol$\cdot\max|y|$. The poles are the zeros of the denominator, the finite eigenvalues of an $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ arrowhead pencil.

Computing $w$ from a fresh SVD at step $n$ costs $O(m n^2)$, so a fit that ends at $N$ support points costs $O(m N^3)$. A box holding dozens of zeros needs $N \sim 100$, where the SVDs can outweigh the evaluations of $f$.

Updating instead of refactoring

Between consecutive steps $L$ changes in two small ways. Choosing sample $p$ as the new support point

  1. removes row $p$, which is a rank-one change, and
  2. appends one column, the Loewner column of $p$.

A QR factorization can follow both changes in $O(mn)$ [2], [3, §6.5]; Lietaert et al. [7] update AAA's Loewner QR the same way. Keep $L = QR$ with $Q \in \mathbb{C}^{m\times n}$ having orthonormal columns and $R$ upper triangular, and let removed rows stay in place as zero rows, so no index bookkeeping is needed.

Zeroing row $p$. Let $q = Q^{H} e_p$ and $u = (e_p - Qq)/\tau$ with $\tau = \lVert e_p - Qq\rVert$. Then $[\,Q\ \ u\,]$ has orthonormal columns, its range contains $e_p$, and its row $p$ is $[\,q^{H}\ \ \tau\,]$. Apply $n$ Givens rotations, each mixing column $j$ with the last column for $j = n,\dots,1$, chosen to move row $p$ onto $[\,0\ \cdots\ 0\ \ 1\,]$. Afterwards the last column is $e_p$ itself, since it has unit norm and a unit entry, and the first $n$ columns $Q'$ vanish in row $p$. The same rotations applied to the rows of $\begin{bmatrix} R \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ give $\begin{bmatrix} R' \\ v^{T} \end{bmatrix}$ with $R'$ still upper triangular, because the order $j = n,\dots,1$ only fills the extra row. So

\[L = Q'R' + e_p v^{T} ,\]

where $v^T$ is the old row $p$ of $L$, and $Q'R'$ is $L$ with that row zeroed. Each rotation touches two columns of length $m$ and two rows of length at most $n$: $O(mn)$ in total.

Appending a column. Orthogonalize the new column $a$ against $Q$ by classical Gram–Schmidt, $r = Q^{H}a$, $a \leftarrow a - Qr$; the coefficients $r$ and the remaining norm form the new column of $R$. Near convergence the new column is almost in the range of $Q$, which is exactly when one pass loses orthogonality, so the pass is repeated while the remainder keeps collapsing; two passes are enough [4]. Cost $O(mn)$.

Weights by inverse iteration

The smallest right singular vector of $L$ is that of $R$, and for a triangular matrix it comes cheaply from inverse iteration on $R^{H}R$ [5]: each sweep $w \leftarrow R^{-1}R^{-H}w$, then normalize, is two triangular solves, $O(n^2)$. A sweep damps every other singular direction by $(\sigma_n/\sigma_j)^2$. Convergence to the singular vector is slow when $\sigma_{n-1}\approx\sigma_n$, but the fit does not need the vector: it needs $\lVert Rw\rVert$ small, and directions that survive the damping have $\sigma_j \approx \sigma_n$ by construction. A fixed handful of sweeps therefore gives weights as good as the SVD's.

The two triangular solves work on $R$ and $R^{H}$ separately rather than on the Gram matrix $L^{H}L$. That matters: a converged fit has $\sigma_n/\sigma_1 \sim$ tol $= 10^{-13}$, and factoring $L^{H}L$ would square it past machine precision. Updating a Cholesky factor of the Gram matrix is the route taken by FastAAA [6], which suits looser tolerances than a zero search can afford.

Scaling

With $m$ samples and a fit ending at $N$ support points:

stagerefactoring every stepupdating
Loewner factorization$O(mN^3)$$O(mN^2)$
weights$O(N^4)$$O(N^3)$
mismatch at the samples$O(mN^2)$$O(mN^2)$
poles, once per fit$O(N^3)$$O(N^3)$

The fit drops from $O(mN^3)$ to $O(mN^2)$, a factor that grows with the number of roots in the box. What remains at large $N$ is the pole eigenproblem.

What the fit can see

A zero $z_0$ of $f$ is a pole of $1/f$ with residue $1/f'(z_0)$. The fit resolves it only if that residue stands above tol$\cdot\max|1/f|$ at the samples it keeps: samples where $1/f$ is zero, non-finite, or above the median of its nearest samples by more than $1/\sqrt{\mathrm{tol}}$ (a sample on a pole of $1/f$) are dropped. Zeros where $|f'|$ is exponentially large are found or missed depending on details of the sampling, so a zero set is reproducible only above this threshold.
